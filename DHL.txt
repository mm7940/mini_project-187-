Good morning everyone,

I’d like to take a few minutes to share my experience working in the Pega team at Standard Chartered — not just from a development standpoint, but how these experiences helped me understand the bigger picture of delivering efficient and scalable solutions.

During my work across multiple user stories, I started noticing a consistent pattern — most challenges were not purely technical, but were centered around how effectively we manage requirements, design solutions, and collaborate as a team.

This became clear to me during one of my initial user stories related to Non-US Charges and report enhancements.

At the start, the requirement appeared straightforward. However, as we progressed, there were multiple changes in scope — such as updates in the number of reports and lack of clarity around critical elements like STP flags, NSTP reasons, and exit criteria.

This led to frequent back-and-forth discussions to understand the actual requirement. While necessary, it introduced delays and, in some cases, changes even came during unit testing, leading to rework.

That experience made me realize that everything starts with clarity.
When requirements are clearly defined with proper scenarios and examples at the beginning, it sets a strong foundation and avoids unnecessary iterations later.

As I moved forward, I encountered another type of challenge — this time not related to clarity, but to efficiency in handling recurring changes.

In multiple user stories, we were updating threshold values for different markets and flows like IDA and ROF. While each change was simple, the pattern was repetitive — these updates were happening frequently.

Every time, even for a small change, we had to go through the full cycle of unit testing, SIT, and UAT. Over time, this became a repetitive effort and impacted overall productivity.

This is where my perspective started shifting.

Instead of treating each change as a new task, I began to see it as a recurring business pattern.
And when something is recurring, the solution should not be repetitive — it should be configurable.

By introducing a configurable or rule-based approach, where threshold values can be managed dynamically, we can significantly reduce development effort, minimize testing cycles, and improve turnaround time.

While these experiences shaped my thinking on design and efficiency, I also observed an important aspect related to team collaboration, especially during the testing phase.

After development, when features moved to SIT and UAT, there were instances where defects were raised directly without prior discussion.

In some cases, these were not actual defects but misunderstandings of expected behavior or scenarios outside the scope of implementation.

At the same time, developers were already working on the next sprint. So addressing these issues led to context switching, which impacted focus and slowed down ongoing development.

This highlighted another key learning for me — delivery is not just about building the right solution, but also about how effectively teams collaborate around it.

A simple improvement, like having a quick validation discussion between tester and developer before raising a defect, can reduce unnecessary cycles, avoid disruptions, and improve overall efficiency.

So if I bring all these experiences together, one clear theme emerges.

Efficient delivery is built on three key pillars:

Clarity at the start
Scalability in design
Collaboration across teams

When these three come together, we not only improve development speed, but also enhance quality, reduce rework, and create a more predictable delivery model.

This shift in perspective — from just completing tasks to improving the way we deliver — has been a key learning for me during my time on this project.

Thank you.
